Survival Curves by Dosage

A simulated 365-day trial of 250 patients in three arms. Filter the patients and compare arms: Kaplan-Meier curves show the share still alive each day, the log-rank test asks whether two curves differ, and the chi-square test asks whether adverse-event rates differ.

  1. Choose patients by gender and age, or switch to Your own data and paste any time-to-event data.
  2. Tick the arms to compare.
  3. Read the curves, then the tests below them. A dropping line means patients dying; the dashed line marks half still alive.
Data

One row per subject: group, time, event. Event is 1 if it happened (death, relapse, a customer cancelling) and 0 if the subject was still fine when follow-up ended (censored). Commas, semicolons or tabs all work, so a spreadsheet column copy pastes straight in. The example is a real, published trial: Freireich and colleagues (1963), weeks of leukemia remission on 6-MP against placebo, where the textbook answer is a log-rank statistic of 16.79. Nothing leaves your browser.

Benford's Law Audit

In many real ledgers about 30% of amounts start with a 1 and under 5% with a 9 (Benford's Law). Made-up amounts often break that pattern. Test a synthetic ledger of 1,200 tax claims with 47 planted anomalies, or paste your own amounts; then tune the risk score that ranks entries for review.

  1. Pick a slice of the ledger, or paste amounts.
  2. Compare the bars (your amounts) with the dots (Benford's Law), and read the fit test.
  3. Move the sliders to reweight the risk score and see how many planted anomalies it catches.

Risk score for review

From Tax Anomaly Audit Compliance Engine (FINDINGS). The ledger is synthetic and seeded. Benford statistics and risk scores are recomputed here; the Isolation Forest scores and tax-rate rule flags are read from the project's run. Every deploy checks the page against the project's Python, including the 30/70 weights and the 43 entries FINDINGS reports.

Fraud Rule Tester

Three simple rules screen 345,524 simulated card transactions, 2,591 of them planted fraud. Loosen or tighten each rule and see the trade-off a fraud team lives with: catching more fraud means more false alarms on real customers.

  1. Load the simulated transactions (about 3.8 MB, once), or switch to Your own transactions and paste or upload a CSV.
  2. Move the sliders: how many charges in how many minutes count as card testing, and how large a purchase counts as a spike.
  3. Watch precision (share of alerts that are fraud) and recall (share of fraud caught) move.
Data

One row per charge: customer, time, amount, then optionally foreign (1 or 0, or a location; blank, 0, no or domestic count as home) and fraud (1 or 0, only to score the rules). Time is a date and time (2026-03-01 14:05) or a number of seconds. Without a fraud column you see the alerts but not precision or recall. The example below is made up to show each rule once; replace it with an export from a bank or card statement. It is read in your browser and never uploaded.

From FinTech Credit Risk & Fraud Command Center (FINDINGS). The transactions are simulated with a fixed seed and the fraud is planted by the simulator; the rules never read the labels. The rules are etl/fraud_rules.py rewritten for the browser; every deploy checks them against the Python at five settings, and through the CSV upload path, and they must agree on every count.

LinkedIn Post Checker

CurioSync drafts LinkedIn posts from project notes and will not queue one until it passes these writing checks: length, a short first line, no stock AI phrases ("delve", "game-changer", "not just X but Y"), no engagement bait, no word or number repeated too often, and paragraphs that each move the story on.

  1. Paste your own draft, or pick a sample: a post CurioSync queued and test drafts written to break the rules.
  2. Edit the text; the checks rerun as you type.
  3. Read what CurioSync would send back for a rewrite.

From CurioSync: writing_problems in app/services/post_rules.py, rewritten for the browser. Its fact checks (a number or tool the project's notes do not back, "we" on solo work) need the project's own facts, so they do not run here; a draft that passes on this page can still be held back by them. Every deploy checks this page against the Python on 15 published and test drafts: the same problems, word for word, in the same order.

Member Q&A by Meaning

Ask about a concierge client in plain words ("Where does Sophia want to fly by private jet?"). The engine finds the member, ranks that member's messages by meaning rather than shared words (sentence embeddings from all-MiniLM-L6-v2), and reads the answer out of the best one. On 26 labelled, reworded questions it found the right message 25 times (96%).

  1. Pick a labelled question or type your own about one of the 10 members, or switch to Your own text and search lines you paste.
  2. Press Ask. The first time downloads the language model and its runtime (about 27 MB).
  3. Read the answer, then the member's messages ranked by similarity.
Search

One item per line: notes, FAQ answers, ticket titles, anything. Up to 300 lines are embedded in your browser and never uploaded. The example lines are made up.

From Member Messages QA System (FINDINGS): answer_question in main.py, rewritten for the browser, on its 100 synthetic concierge messages. The page runs the 8-bit copy of the same model; the project's spaCy name step does not run in a browser, so the member is found by its first-name fallback, which picks the same member on all 26 labelled questions. Every deploy checks that all 26 give Python's member, top message and answer.

Customer Segments by RFM

Each customer becomes three numbers: days since their last purchase (recency), how many purchases (frequency) and total spend (monetary). K-Means groups customers with similar numbers. With 4 groups the project's 2,000 customers split into VIP Champions, Loyal, New and At Risk, and the groups explain 93.9% of the variance.

  1. Move the slider to try 2 to 8 clusters and watch the elbow chart: each extra cluster explains less.
  2. Read each segment's size and average recency, purchases and spend.
  3. Switch to Your own transactions and load a CSV, or try the 60-customer sample.
Customers

A CSV with one row per purchase: customer, date, amount (a header row is fine). Dates like 2025-03-01 or 2025-03-01 14:05. The file is read in your browser and never uploaded.

Land-Use Emissions Forecast

EPA's state inventory tracks how much CO2e each kind of land releases or absorbs every year. Pick a state and land type to see 1990-2022, and how three forecasts did on 2018-2022, each made from the three years before it. Across all 25 series the linear regression scores 94.2% accuracy, but simply repeating last year's value scores 94.7%. Rerun from eight starting years (2015-2022), no model beat last year's value in 6 of the 8 (findings).

  1. Pick a state and a land type.
  2. Compare the forecast lines with the actual values in the shaded test years.
  3. Switch to Your own yearly series and paste any numbers by year to run the same test on them.
Series

One line per year: year, value. At least 11 years in a row. The example is made up; replace it with your own (sales, rainfall, enrolment, anything yearly). Nothing is uploaded.

From Multi-State Land Use Emissions Analysis (FINDINGS): models/epa_forecast.py on EPA's Inventory of U.S. Greenhouse Gas Emissions and Sinks by State: 1990-2022, land use and forestry sector (public domain). The linear regression is refitted in your browser; the 300-tree Random Forest's predictions come from Python. Every deploy checks that all 125 test predictions match scikit-learn's and that the three accuracies come out the same.
