Trained Models, Running in Your Browser

These are the projects' own trained models, not re-creations. They download only when you press Run, then run on this page; nothing you draw or show them leaves your device.

Shape Classifier

A small CNN that names the shape in a 64 x 64 image (circle, square, triangle, rectangle, pentagon, hexagon or star) and draws a box around it. It scored 99.20% on 2,000 held-out images. Next to it, the project's classic image-processing path turns the picture black and white, cleans it and reads the outline's corners, which gives a second, independent answer.

  1. Pick a test shape or one it never saw, draw your own, or upload any picture: a road sign, a logo, a photo.
  2. Press Run the model. The first run downloads the model, ONNX Runtime and OpenCV.js (about 6 MB).
  3. Follow the processing steps, compare the two answers, and read whether the answer can be trusted.
Input

Held-out test shapes (the 7 it knows)

Shapes it never saw (octagon, heptagon, ellipse, plus sign, arrow, crescent)

Draw one filled shape with your finger or mouse. Your drawing is not like the training images (those are clean, filled and slightly noisy), so expect lower accuracy here; the 99.20% applies only to the test shapes.

Your picture is read in your browser and never uploaded or saved. It is padded to a square and shrunk to 64 x 64, the only size the model takes, so fine detail (a sign's lettering) is lost. A single dark shape on a plain light background is closest to what the model was trained on; anything else is a real-world test of how it fails.

How the picture was processed (src/traditional.py, the same OpenCV steps in your browser)

Steps 2 to 6 feed the geometry answer only. The CNN reads step 1 directly: it learned its own filters, so it never sees the black-and-white mask. When the two answers disagree, one of them is wrong.

The model and test images come from Solid Object Detection: the same ONNX file it serves on AWS Lambda, and 9 images of each shape from its seed-42 held-out split (FINDINGS). For a test shape, the page also shows Python's score for the same image, and every deploy checks the model's scores and every geometry step against Python. The trust check and its numbers come from the project's ood_check.py, which tested the model on 1,200 shapes it never saw.

ASL Word Signs

Recognises 29 STEM and computing signs (computer, email, science, math...) from about two seconds of video. On 58 held-out clips the right sign was the top guess 75.86% of the time and in the top three 94.83% of the time (chance is 3.45%).

  1. Pick a held-out clip, or switch to Use your camera and sign one of the words listed below.
  2. Press Recognise the sign.
  3. Read the top three signs. The picture shows the hand points the model reads, frame by frame.
Input

The camera runs only on this page: frames are read in your browser and are never uploaded or saved. The first start downloads MediaPipe's hand model and OpenCV.js (about 14 MB). Sign with your hands fully in view and good light; a phone works, but more slowly.

Signs it knows: computer, internet, email, program, network, machine, print, television, write, letter, paper, test, learn, study, teacher, school, science, math, calculate, research, measure, plus, energy, chemistry, biology, physics, experiment, book, picture. See how each is signed in the WLASL dataset.

The model and the held-out clips come from Extending STEM across ASL (FINDINGS): the same 1-D convolution and GRU, trained on the same seeded split, with the clips stored as hand points only (WLASL video is for research use, so none is copied here). Every deploy checks this page's OpenCV.js step and model scores against Python's; MediaPipe's browser and Python versions were checked on 7 hand photos and agree to 0.00003 of the frame.
